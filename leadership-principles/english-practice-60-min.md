# English speaking practice — Vitória's 60-minute session

**For the teacher:** This is a speaking lesson about workplace experiences. You can conduct the entire session without knowing anything about technology or Amazon. The eight main questions combine the 16 Leadership Principles behind this study material; you can simply read the questions and follow-ups below. An optional, collapsed map at the end explains the connections for Vitória.

A Solutions Architect helps a company plan technology changes, understand the choices involved, and explain their effect on people and the business. The sample answers use everyday language to describe this work.

**About the examples:** Every sample answer, company, event, and number below is fictional. The speaker is a Principal-level Solutions Architect who influences several teams, explains difficult choices, develops others, and makes improvements that last. Vitória should answer with her own experience; if she uses an imagined situation for language practice, she should say so. The teacher should assess how clearly she communicates, without expecting her experience to match the examples.

## The one-hour plan

Use **Q01–Q08** for this session. **R01–R08** are reserve questions for another lesson or to replace a question when a different topic would be more useful.

| Elapsed time (minutes:seconds) | Activity |

| 04:00–10:00 | Q01 — Supporting a decision you disagreed with |
| 10:00–16:00 | Q02 — Finding the real customer problem |
| 16:00–22:00 | Q03 — Helping someone grow |
| 22:00–28:00 | Q04 — Acting quickly while protecting quality |
| 28:00–34:00 | Q05 — Changing your mind after learning something new |
| 34:00–40:00 | Q06 — Taking responsibility and following through |

| 46:00–52:00 | Q08 — Thinking bigger and considering the wider impact |
| 52:00–60:00 | Language feedback and a short second attempt at one answer |

For each six-minute slot, aim for a two- to three-minute first answer, one follow-up, and a short restatement. Use any remaining time for a second follow-up or transition. If you need to substitute a reserve question, the coverage map shows which main questions to revisit later; the original eight-question route is the one that covers all 16 principles.

**Warm-up question:** “Could you introduce yourself and describe your work to someone who has never worked in your field?”

**Warm-up follow-up:** “What is one problem you help people solve?”

## How to conduct the conversation

Read the question, let Vitória answer, and choose one follow-up. Keep the sample answer collapsed until she has finished. The examples are available for comparison after the attempt or after class; reading them aloud is a separate exercise.

Listen for a clear explanation of the problem, her responsibility, what she did, and what happened. If an unfamiliar word prevents you from following the story, use one of these prompts:

- “What does that mean in everyday English?”
- “Could you give me a simple example?”
- “What did you do personally?”
- “Why did you choose that option?”
- “What happened next?”
- “Could you explain the same idea in one sentence?”

Let her finish the initial answer and keep notes for the feedback section. You can ask for clarification at any point when the meaning is unclear. Evaluate intelligibility and communication rather than how closely her answer resembles the model.

## Q01 — Supporting a decision you disagreed with

**Question:** Tell me about a time you disagreed with an important decision but still helped carry out the final plan. How did you explain your concerns and maintain trust?

**Follow-ups — choose one:**

- What did you say when your preferred option was not chosen?
- How did you show support through your actions afterward?

**Short restatement:** “In two sentences, explain what you disagreed with and what you did after the decision.”

<details>
<summary>Fictional sample answer — open after Vitória's attempt</summary>

**Situation:** I was advising a bank on a new account-opening service. Three teams were involved, and the business director wanted to keep an announced launch date. I recommended a two-week delay because the support team had not practiced handling common customer problems.

**Task:** My responsibility was to explain the risk clearly and help the client make an informed decision. I could recommend a direction, but the bank owned the final decision.

**Action:** I brought examples of situations that support staff could not yet resolve. I also acknowledged that I had raised the training concern later than I should have. Rather than arguing that my plan was the only acceptable one, I compared the options and their consequences.

The director chose to keep the date and launch to a smaller group of customers. That was not my preferred option, but it retained the essential quality checks and gave support a manageable workload. Once the decision was made, I helped prepare a short support guide, arranged practice sessions, and agreed on daily updates. I explained the final plan consistently, including when I spoke with people who knew I had disagreed.

**Result:** The limited launch stayed within the agreed support capacity. After three weeks of stable operation, the bank expanded it. The teams knew both what I had questioned and what I was committed to delivering.

**Reflection:** I learned to raise concerns earlier and to make my support visible after a decision. Trust depended on being candid before the decision and reliable afterward.

**Useful language:** “That was not my preferred option, but…” / “Once the decision was made, I…”

</details>

## Q02 — Finding the real customer problem

**Question:** Tell me about a time customers were unhappy even though the reports looked good. How did you find out what was really happening, and what did you change?

**Follow-ups — choose one:**

- What did you discover by listening to customers?
- How did you know the change helped?

**Short restatement:** “Explain the problem as if I were one of the customers.”

<details>
<summary>Fictional sample answer — open after Vitória's attempt</summary>

**Situation:** A bank's application was completing most requests quickly, yet customers kept complaining about opening an account. The project team believed the problem was simply that people did not understand the instructions.

**Task:** As the lead architect advising several delivery teams, I needed to establish what customers were experiencing before recommending another change.

**Action:** I reviewed support calls with the customer-service team and asked to see a sample of incomplete applications. I then compared people who finished the process with people who stopped halfway through it.

The reports measured how quickly each individual step worked. They did not show how many times a person had to repeat a step. Customers whose identity document was rejected received the same vague message, even when the problem was something simple, such as a blurred photograph.

I proposed clearer messages and an easier way to try again, while keeping the identity checks. The client's design and development teams built the changes. I helped them agree on a better measure: the share of people who completed the whole process, including those who needed a second attempt.

**Result:** In a four-week trial, completion rose from 64% to 76% for comparable groups of applicants. Related support calls also fell. We checked both measures because fewer calls alone could have meant that customers had given up. The bank added the full customer journey to its regular reviews.

**Reflection:** I would involve customer-service staff at the beginning next time. They had seen the pattern before it appeared in the project reports.

**Useful language:** “The reports showed…, but they did not show…” / “We checked both measures because…”

</details>

## Q03 — Helping someone grow

**Question:** Tell me about a time you helped someone take on more responsibility while also improving the way the team worked.

**Follow-ups — choose one:**

- How did you support the person without doing the work for them?
- What changed for the rest of the team?

**Short restatement:** “Explain how you knew the person was becoming more independent.”

<details>
<summary>Fictional sample answer — open after Vitória's attempt</summary>

**Situation:** In a group supporting several banking clients, the same two experienced architects handled nearly every difficult customer conversation. Less experienced colleagues rarely had the chance to lead, and the senior people were becoming a bottleneck.

**Task:** I wanted to help a colleague develop the confidence and judgment to lead independently. I also wanted to make the team's workload more sustainable.

**Action:** I first asked her which responsibilities she wanted to develop. She was comfortable solving problems but found it difficult to respond when senior customers challenged her recommendation.

We agreed on a six-week plan. She prepared and led a small part of a customer meeting, and I observed. Afterward, I asked her to assess what had gone well before I gave feedback. We practiced difficult questions, but I avoided giving her a script to memorize.

I also changed the team process. We rotated meeting leadership, shared preparation notes, and made time for less experienced colleagues to contribute before senior people offered their views. For high-risk decisions, an experienced architect remained available to help.

**Result:** By the end of the six weeks, she was leading routine customer reviews and knew when to ask for support. Two more colleagues later followed the same development plan. The experienced architects no longer had to attend every meeting.

**Reflection:** Her progress came from her preparation and practice. My contribution was to provide opportunities, specific feedback, and a safer environment for learning. Giving someone responsibility worked best when I also changed the conditions around them.

**Useful language:** “We agreed on a six-week plan.” / “My contribution was to provide…”

</details>

## Q04 — Acting quickly while protecting quality

**Question:** Tell me about a time you had to act quickly without having all the information. How did you decide what was safe to do and what still needed checking?

**Follow-ups — choose one:**

- What could you change back if your decision was wrong?
- What would have made you stop or ask for help?

**Short restatement:** “Explain why your quick action was a reasonable risk.”

<details>
<summary>Fictional sample answer — open after Vitória's attempt</summary>

**Situation:** During a bank's busy morning period, a recent change caused some customers to see an error when checking their balances. We did not yet know the exact cause, but the problem had started shortly after the change.

**Task:** I was advising the response team. My immediate goal was to help restore a reliable service without creating a bigger problem by making an untested change.

**Action:** I separated the urgent decision from the longer investigation. I asked whether we could return the affected part of the service to its previous version. We confirmed that the change only affected how information was displayed; it had not altered payments or account records. That distinction mattered because changing the display back would not undo a financial transaction.

The client's operations lead approved the action. Before expanding it, we checked it with a small group and compared the displayed balances with the bank's account records. We agreed to stop if those checks failed. Another team prepared a clear customer update so that people knew their account records had not been affected.

**Result:** The service returned to its normal error level within 25 minutes. We continued the investigation afterward and added a check for the condition that had caused the problem. The same check became part of the release process used by four teams.

**Reflection:** Acting quickly did not require pretending we understood everything. It required knowing the limits of the action, who could approve it, and which evidence would tell us to stop.

**Useful language:** “We did not yet know…, but we had confirmed…” / “We agreed to stop if…”

</details>

## Q05 — Changing your mind after learning something new

**Question:** Tell me about a time you had to learn something unfamiliar and then change a recommendation you had already made. What changed your mind?

**Follow-ups — choose one:**

- How did you check whether your original assumption was wrong?
- How did you explain the change to other people?

**Short restatement:** “Complete this explanation: ‘At first I thought…, but after…, I recommended…’”

<details>
<summary>Fictional sample answer — open after Vitória's attempt</summary>

**Situation:** I initially recommended that a banking group use one shared process for a service used in three countries. I had seen that approach reduce duplicated work elsewhere, and I expected it to work again.

**Task:** Before the client committed to the plan, I needed to check whether the operational differences between the countries would change the recommendation. I had limited experience with how those local teams handled exceptions.

**Action:** I asked an operations specialist to explain the process to me and spent time with the people handling unusual customer cases. I also asked a colleague who disagreed with my recommendation to identify the assumption most likely to fail.

Together, we tested a small set of realistic cases from each country. The normal cases were similar, but exception handling was substantially different. My original plan would have forced local teams into frequent manual work.

I changed the recommendation to share the common steps while keeping the local exception processes separate. I explained to the client's leadership that the original proposal had underestimated those differences. I showed which parts of the analysis had changed, rather than presenting the new recommendation as if it had always been my plan.

**Result:** The client accepted the revised design before implementation began. Two country teams later reused the common steps successfully, while retaining their own exception handling. The third team was still preparing its adoption, so I did not count that as a completed result.

**Reflection:** Experience helped me form a starting hypothesis. Learning from people closer to the work helped me decide whether it actually applied.

**Useful language:** “I had expected…, but the evidence showed…” / “The original proposal had underestimated…”

</details>

## Q06 — Taking responsibility and following through

**Question:** Tell me about a time an important piece of work had no clear owner and put a promised result at risk. What did you take responsibility for, and what was actually delivered?

**Follow-ups — choose one:**

- How did you balance this work with your existing responsibilities?
- Did you meet the original goal? Please explain any gap.

**Short restatement:** “Separate your own contribution from the team's result.”

<details>
<summary>Fictional sample answer — open after Vitória's attempt</summary>

**Situation:** A banking group was preparing to introduce a new customer service across four business units. Each team had completed its own work, but no one owned the process for handing unresolved customer cases from one team to another.

**Task:** My formal role was to advise on the overall design. However, without an agreed handover, the bank could not deliver its promise to reduce the average time to resolve those cases from 20 days to 10.

**Action:** I raised the gap with the programme director and took responsibility for coordinating a solution. I renegotiated another assignment so that this work had explicit time allocated to it.

I brought the four teams together to agree who would receive each type of case, what information they needed, and when a case should be escalated. The team leaders remained responsible for their staff and daily operations. I tested the handover with sample cases, tracked unresolved decisions, and made sure a permanent owner was appointed before I stepped back.

**Result:** The service launched with the handover process in place. After two months, the average resolution time was 12 days. That was a substantial improvement, but it missed the original 10-day target. I reported the gap and helped the new owner identify the remaining delays. We did not redefine the target to make the result look better.

**Reflection:** Taking responsibility meant getting the work completed and leaving clear ownership behind. It also meant being honest about a result that was useful but still below the original goal.

**Useful language:** “My formal role was…, but…” / “We improved…, although we did not reach…”

</details>

## Q07 — Making work simpler with limited resources

**Question:** Tell me about a time you made a complicated piece of work simpler when you had limited time, money, or people. How did you check that you had actually reduced the effort?

**Follow-ups — choose one:**

- What did you reuse instead of building something new?
- Did the change create extra work for anyone else?

**Short restatement:** “Explain the before-and-after difference without using technical terms.”

<details>
<summary>Fictional sample answer — open after Vitória's attempt</summary>

**Situation:** Several teams in a consulting group prepared similar technology reviews for banking clients. Each team used a different document, and senior architects spent hours asking for missing information. We had six weeks to improve the process and no budget for another tool.

**Task:** I wanted to reduce repeated work while keeping the review useful for clients with different needs. A single, lengthy form would have made the process more consistent but harder to use.

**Action:** I observed three teams carrying out reviews and asked which questions actually changed their recommendations. We found that much of the paperwork repeated information already available elsewhere.

I proposed a short common checklist and optional sections for unusual risks. We reused an existing document system and added a simple check for missing information. Two teams tried the approach first, including one that was skeptical about standardisation.

I counted preparation, review, and correction time together. This helped us check that we were not simply making the reviewers' lives easier by giving the delivery teams more work.

**Result:** Across the first 18 comparable reviews, the median total effort fell from eight hours to five. The initial improvement took about 40 hours of work, so I reported that cost alongside the ongoing saving. The checks for important risks remained in place, and six teams adopted the process over the following quarter.

**Reflection:** The valuable invention was a simpler way of working. Buying another tool would not have solved the underlying duplication.

**Useful language:** “We reused… rather than starting from scratch.” / “I counted… together, so that…”

</details>

## Q08 — Thinking bigger and considering the wider impact

**Question:** Tell me about a time you proposed a bigger change, then adjusted the plan because of its effects on people beyond the project. How did you keep the ambition while addressing those concerns?

**Follow-ups — choose one:**

- Who might have been affected but was missing from the original discussion?
- What did you change after hearing their perspective?

**Short restatement:** “Explain both the benefit you wanted and the consequence you needed to avoid.”

<details>
<summary>Fictional sample answer — open after Vitória's attempt</summary>

**Situation:** A regional bank asked me to help improve one online service. The same customer information was being collected repeatedly across several services, so I proposed a broader approach: let customers provide it once and reuse it where appropriate.

**Task:** I needed to show a practical route to a better experience while considering what a wider shift to online services could mean for customers and the surrounding communities.

**Action:** I worked with the bank's business leaders to connect the proposal to three services, starting with one small trial. During planning, I learned that a separate cost-reduction proposal assumed online adoption would allow several branches to close.

I asked the bank to include branch staff and community representatives in the discussion. They described people with limited internet access and local businesses that still depended on in-person help. A successful online trial would not, by itself, establish that those needs had disappeared.

I recommended keeping assisted service available and separating the technology trial from any branch-closure decision. We also added measures for completion with staff assistance, rather than measuring online completion alone.

**Result:** In an eight-week trial, customers completed the selected application in an average of 18 minutes instead of 30. Assisted applications remained available. The bank approved the next service, but required evidence about access and local needs before considering changes to its branch network.

**Reflection:** The broader vision remained valuable. It became more responsible when the plan accounted for people who were not represented in the original project meeting.

**Useful language:** “A successful trial would not, by itself, show that…” / “We kept the broader goal while changing…”

</details>

## Language feedback — the final eight minutes

The teacher can use these questions to guide feedback. Specific examples from Vitória's answers are more useful than a general score.

| Area | What to listen for | One example or correction |
|---|---|---|
| Understanding and relevance | Did she understand the question and address what was asked? | |
| Organisation | Could you follow the problem, her role, the actions, and the outcome? | |
| Fluency | Could she continue speaking, pause naturally, and recover when she lost a word? | |
| Grammar | Were past events, earlier events, and reflections expressed clearly? Listen for verb tense, articles, agreement, and prepositions. | |
| Vocabulary and explanation | Could she choose precise words and explain unfamiliar ideas in everyday language? | |
| Pronunciation and intelligibility | Could you understand key words, numbers, and sentence stress without repeated effort? | |
| Interaction | Did she answer follow-ups directly, ask for clarification when needed, and adapt to the listener? | |

Suggested use of the eight minutes:

1. **Two minutes:** identify two things she communicated well, with examples.
2. **Three minutes:** choose up to three recurring language issues and practise a corrected sentence for each.
3. **Two minutes:** ask her to answer one question again in about 90 seconds, using the feedback.
4. **One minute:** agree on one specific goal for the next lesson.

Useful reflection prompts:

- “Which explanation was easiest to follow, and why?”
- “Which sentence could you make shorter?”
- “How could you express that idea if you forgot the technical word?”
- “What would you change if you were answering the question again?”

## Reserve questions — use for substitutions or another lesson

Each reserve question also has a fictional model answer. These are additional options, rather than extra questions to fit into the same hour.

### R01 — Correcting your own mistake

**Question:** Tell me about a time you discovered that an important recommendation you had made was wrong. How did you correct it and explain the mistake?

**Follow-ups — choose one:**

- What had you assumed without checking?
- What did you change to prevent the same mistake?

<details>
<summary>Fictional sample answer — open after Vitória's attempt</summary>

**Situation:** I recommended an option for a group of banking clients because its expected operating cost was lower. During a later review, I realised I had left out the additional support hours it would require.

**Task:** I needed to correct the comparison before clients made decisions based on it and take responsibility for the error.

**Action:** I checked the missing assumptions with the support team, recalculated the total cost, and asked a colleague to challenge the revised comparison. The option was still suitable for some clients, but it was no longer the cheapest choice in every case.

I informed my manager and contacted the people who had received the original recommendation. I explained what I had missed, which conclusions had changed, and which remained valid. I avoided describing it as a minor update because the mistake could affect a real decision.

I also introduced a review of ongoing support effort before our team published future cost comparisons.

**Result:** Three clients received corrected recommendations before signing off on their plans. One changed its preferred option. The team adopted the additional review, which made the assumptions easier to question.

**Reflection:** I had treated an infrastructure estimate as if it represented the full operating cost. Owning that error helped restore confidence more than trying to defend the original answer would have.

</details>

### R02 — Turning a small improvement into a wider opportunity

**Question:** Tell me about a small improvement that led you to see a much bigger opportunity. How did you test the idea before asking others to invest in it?

**Follow-ups — choose one:**

- What made you think other teams had the same problem?
- What did you leave out of the first version?

<details>
<summary>Fictional sample answer — open after Vitória's attempt</summary>

**Situation:** While helping one bank prepare for a service interruption, I noticed that teams used different contact lists and often disagreed about who should make the first decision.

**Task:** I saw an opportunity to create a common preparation method that several client teams could use. I wanted evidence of its value before proposing a large programme.

**Action:** I interviewed colleagues supporting other banks and found the same coordination problem. I then created a one-page guide covering responsibilities, first actions, and customer communication. I deliberately left out complex scenarios from the first version.

Two teams tested the guide in practice exercises. I observed where they hesitated and revised the wording with them. When I presented the broader proposal, I showed the improvement from those exercises and explained where the guide still needed adaptation.

**Result:** In the second exercise, both teams identified the person responsible for the first decision in under five minutes, compared with more than 15 minutes before. Four additional client teams adopted adapted versions over the next quarter. That was evidence from exercises, so I did not present it as proof of performance during a real emergency.

**Reflection:** The bigger opportunity came from recognising a repeated need. Starting with a simple version helped us learn before committing to a wider rollout.

</details>

### R03 — Making your own involvement less necessary

**Question:** Tell me about a time you took responsibility for a difficult problem and then helped others handle similar problems without depending on you.

**Follow-ups — choose one:**

- What did you teach, and what did you leave for others to decide?
- How did you know the new arrangement was working?

<details>
<summary>Fictional sample answer — open after Vitória's attempt</summary>

**Situation:** Several customer teams asked me to resolve the same type of difficult design decision. I had helped establish the original approach, so people assumed every exception needed my approval.

**Task:** I needed to resolve the immediate cases and remove the dependency that I had partly created.

**Action:** I grouped the recent cases by the judgment they required and found that most followed a few common patterns. I worked with two colleagues to document the reasoning, including situations where the usual approach would be inappropriate.

We reviewed real, anonymised cases together. At first, I asked them to explain their recommendations before I offered mine. Later, they led the discussions and involved me only when a case fell outside the agreed boundaries.

I agreed the new responsibilities with the team leaders and kept a short weekly review for the first month. That gave people support without requiring me to approve every decision.

**Result:** Over the following six weeks, the colleagues handled 14 of 18 cases independently. Four needed additional advice, which was appropriate given their complexity. Customer work no longer waited for my calendar.

**Reflection:** Taking responsibility included recognising when my own involvement had become an obstacle. The lasting improvement was helping other people develop judgment, rather than becoming faster at answering every question myself.

</details>

### R04 — Working within limits without exhausting the team

**Question:** Tell me about a time resources were limited and the team was under pressure. How did you protect both the result and the people doing the work?

**Follow-ups — choose one:**

- What did you decide to stop or postpone?
- How did you check that the workload became more manageable?

<details>
<summary>Fictional sample answer — open after Vitória's attempt</summary>

**Situation:** A small architecture group was supporting three client projects at once. We could not add people that quarter, and two colleagues were repeatedly staying late to prepare similar material.

**Task:** I wanted to preserve the commitments that mattered most without treating longer working hours as our permanent solution.

**Action:** I reviewed the requests with the team and separated time-sensitive client decisions from material that could wait. I brought the capacity problem to the group manager and proposed reducing lower-value internal work.

With the client's agreement, we reused an existing workshop format and prepared the common material once. We kept separate discussions for each client's specific risks. I also asked colleagues which tasks were interrupting them most and helped introduce protected preparation time.

The project owners approved the revised priorities. We were explicit about which internal documents would arrive later, rather than quietly relying on people to finish everything at night.

**Result:** The three scheduled client workshops took place. Over the next month, late working sessions became less frequent, and the team reported a more manageable workload. We did postpone two internal documents, and I included that trade-off in the review.

**Reflection:** Resourcefulness meant choosing work more carefully and reusing what already helped. It also meant acknowledging that limited capacity required a real change in priorities.

</details>

### R05 — Noticing who was being left behind

**Question:** Tell me about a change that worked well for most people but created difficulties for a smaller group. How did you notice and respond?

**Follow-ups — choose one:**

- How did you hear from people who were missing from the main reports?
- What did you change beyond the immediate fix?

<details>
<summary>Fictional sample answer — open after Vitória's attempt</summary>

**Situation:** A bank simplified an application by asking people to upload documents through their phones. Overall completion improved, but staff at branches in rural areas reported that some customers could no longer finish the process.

**Task:** I was advising on the wider design and needed to understand whether the improvement was creating a new barrier to access.

**Action:** I asked the team to examine completion by connection quality and to speak with branch staff. Customers with unreliable internet connections had to restart whenever an upload failed. Some depended on local businesses or relatives to get online, which added time and exposed personal information to more people.

I helped the bank compare ways to preserve the easier process while reducing those consequences. The delivery team added a way to resume an interrupted application. The bank also kept an assisted route for people who needed help.

We included those customer groups in later trials and added their completion rates to the review, so that the average result would not hide a recurring problem.

**Result:** In the next four-week trial, more customers on unreliable connections completed the application. The bank retained the assisted route and applied the same review approach to two other services.

**Reflection:** A better average did not establish that everyone was better served. Listening to the smaller group changed both the solution and how we assessed future changes.

</details>

### R06 — Fixing a recurring problem

**Question:** Tell me about a problem that kept coming back even after people said it was fixed. How did you find the underlying cause and stop the pattern?

**Follow-ups — choose one:**

- What evidence changed your understanding of the problem?
- How did you verify that the improvement lasted?

<details>
<summary>Fictional sample answer — open after Vitória's attempt</summary>

**Situation:** A bank's daily management reports sometimes contained duplicate entries. Teams corrected the reports manually and closed each issue, but the same problem returned twice in a month.

**Task:** I needed to help the teams understand the pattern and agree on a lasting quality check across the process.

**Action:** I followed a small set of duplicated entries from their original source to the final report. I found that, after a delay, one team sometimes sent the same information again. The receiving system treated it as new information each time.

I brought both teams together and showed the sequence using one plain example. We agreed how the receiving system would recognise information it had already processed. The development teams implemented the change, while I helped define the tests and the business checks needed before release.

We tested the exact sequence that had caused the duplicate entries and introduced a daily comparison with the source records. The permanent report owner took responsibility for following up on differences.

**Result:** No duplicates were detected during the next six weeks of daily checks. That observation supported the improvement, although we continued monitoring rather than claiming the problem could never happen again.

**Reflection:** The repeated manual corrections had addressed the visible result. Following the information through the whole process revealed why the problem returned.

</details>

### R07 — Learning from a disagreement

**Question:** Tell me about a disagreement that made you learn something important. How did you use that learning and support the final decision?

**Follow-ups — choose one:**

- What did the other person understand that you had missed?
- Which part of the final decision was different from your preference?

<details>
<summary>Fictional sample answer — open after Vitória's attempt</summary>

**Situation:** I recommended replacing an old internal tool because I believed its awkward design was slowing staff down. An operations manager disagreed and argued that a replacement would create a larger training problem during a busy period.

**Task:** I needed to understand that concern properly and help the client choose a practical direction.

**Action:** I asked the manager to walk me through a normal working day. I discovered that experienced staff used shortcuts that our project team had not documented. I also learned how much time new staff needed to become confident with unfamiliar tools.

I still believed replacement was the better long-term option, but I revised my estimate of the short-term effort. I presented the updated comparison, including what I had learned and where I continued to disagree.

The client decided to improve the existing tool for the next six months and review replacement afterward. I supported that choice by helping prioritise the most useful changes and define the evidence for the later review. I did not keep reopening the argument in delivery meetings.

**Result:** The team introduced the selected improvements without disrupting the busy period. The client also had a documented basis for the next decision, instead of having to repeat the original debate.

**Reflection:** Understanding a different perspective improved my recommendation, even though it did not remove every disagreement.

</details>

### R08 — Delivering something useful under time pressure

**Question:** Tell me about a time an unexpected obstacle threatened an important deadline. What did you do quickly, what did you leave for later, and what result did you deliver?

**Follow-ups — choose one:**

- How did you decide what was essential for the deadline?
- What did the first delivery still not solve?

<details>
<summary>Fictional sample answer — open after Vitória's attempt</summary>

**Situation:** A client needed to train staff in five regions before launching a new service. Six days before the training, we learned that the planned practice environment would not be ready.

**Task:** I needed to help preserve the training's most important outcome: staff should recognise common customer problems and know what to do first.

**Action:** I asked the training lead which skills required the live practice environment and which could be taught another way. We chose a small set of realistic customer cases and built a guided exercise using material we already had.

I tested the exercise with two staff members the next day and changed the confusing instructions. The client approved a limited first session focused on those cases. We kept hands-on system practice as a separate, clearly scheduled follow-up, rather than suggesting the first session covered everything.

**Result:** All five regional groups completed the case-based session before the service launch. Staff could identify the first action for the cases we had practised. The hands-on session took place two weeks later, so that part missed the original training date.

**Reflection:** Moving quickly helped us deliver a useful part of the outcome. Being precise about the remaining gap was essential to planning the next step.

</details>

The order is deliberately mixed. Each main question combines two principles, so Q01–Q08 together cover all 16. The links are for further study; the lesson is self-contained.

| Main question | Principles combined | What the fictional answer illustrates |
|---|---|---|
| Q01 | [Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit](13-have-backbone-disagree-and-commit.md) + [Earn Trust](11-earn-trust.md) | Respectful challenge, candid communication, and visible support after a decision. |
| Q02 | [Customer Obsession](01-customer-obsession.md) + [Dive Deep](12-dive-deep.md) | Listening to users and investigating a gap between reports and real experience. |
| Q03 | [Hire and Develop the Best](06-hire-and-develop-the-best.md) + [Strive to be Earth’s Best Employer](15-strive-to-be-earths-best-employer.md) | Coaching, increasing independence, and changing working conditions for the wider team. |
| Q04 | [Bias for Action](09-bias-for-action.md) + [Insist on the Highest Standards](07-insist-on-the-highest-standards.md) | An urgent, bounded action with clear checks and a lasting quality improvement. |

| Q06 | [Ownership](02-ownership.md) + [Deliver Results](14-deliver-results.md) | Taking responsibility across teams, following through, and reporting a result below target honestly. |
| Q07 | [Invent and Simplify](03-invent-and-simplify.md) + [Frugality](10-frugality.md) | Removing duplicated work, reusing existing resources, and measuring total effort. |

The Principal-level scope in these examples comes from the breadth of the work: advising leaders, influencing teams without managing them, taking a longer-term view, teaching others, and creating practices other teams can use. The speaker explains this responsibility in plain English and distinguishes personal decisions from the team's implementation and results.

For your own response, keep your actual scope and contribution. A clear example from a smaller project is more useful for this lesson than borrowing the fictional speaker's position, numbers, or achievements.
